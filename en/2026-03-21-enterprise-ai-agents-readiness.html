Enterprise AI agents: define the job and who takes it back

8 min read

Compare benchmark completion criteria with Frontier and AgentCore Policy, then use an address-change scenario to connect outcomes, permissions, exception ownership, and review cost before expanding adoption.

The enterprise adoption question

After the successful demo, who takes the work back?

A customer asks to change a delivery address. An agent finds the order and saves the new address. For an ordinary order, the demonstration ends quickly. Can it also finish the job when the order has shipped, the new address is incomplete, or a connection drops before the save result is known? This is a hypothetical scenario for examining adoption decisions.

The question is which jobs an organization can delegate, under which conditions. Receiving an address-change request, drafting a change, saving it to an order, and notifying the warehouse can start from similar requests while requiring different permissions and completion criteria. Treating one successful example as success across a department hides the work that remains.

Public evaluations offer task shapes worth testing. Enterprise products offer ways to connect that work and restrict execution. This article compares those contributions, then links evidence of completion to the person who takes over when execution fails. Its recommendation is to expand delegation within the conditions that this relationship has actually established.

Learning from public evaluations

Code changes, browser actions, and customer support need different checks

The first SWE-bench paper evaluates code changes for GitHub issues through tests. It checks tests that should move from failing to passing and tests that previously passed. This returns to behavior after the change, beyond a statement that a fix was made. Passing those tests still does not establish deployment readiness or every effect on users.

WebArena v2 likewise emphasizes whether execution achieves the intended goal over textual agreement between action sequences. In an example involving research, route planning, and writing to a specified repository, it checks the actual contents. That view of outcomes can inform an organization's browser tasks. Its self-hosted sites and resettable initial states, however, do not directly establish sustained operation on changing production websites.

The first τ-bench paper combines user dialogue, domain rules, and tool calls, evaluating final data and required responses. It contains an important qualification: a correct final database can receive a successful reward even when an action omitted explicit user confirmation. Correct outcomes and execution through permitted procedures require separate checks.

These evaluations measure different tasks. An organization needs to translate them into the outputs and saved results to inspect, existing behavior to preserve, and permissions required before execution. When those conditions differ, a public success rate is not the organization's success rate. The useful transfer is often a previously overlooked check rather than a ranking.

Changes in enterprise products

Understanding the work and restricting execution serve different responsibilities

Applying evaluation conditions to an organization requires handling dispersed information and permissions. OpenAI's February 5 announcement of Frontier describes shared business context across CRM, ticketing, and internal applications, combined with execution, evaluation, agent identities, and permissions. Availability is limited to selected customers. These are vendor product descriptions; they do not establish the same quality or adoption benefits in another organization.

Amazon Bedrock AgentCore Policy, generally available from March 3, addresses another responsibility. It manages tool access and input rules outside agent code. A Gateway with an associated policy engine intercepts requests and evaluates whether to allow or deny access. This release concerns Policy; related features should not all be assigned the same availability status.

This article interprets the two approaches as reasons to design business information and execution rules separately during adoption. Business context helps find the right order. Permission to change it also depends on the requester, target, and shipping status. Sharing information or allowing a call alone does not establish that an address change is correct.

Connecting departments to a common product also leaves the meaning of their terms to check. Customer support might use “change complete” to mean receipt of a request, while a warehouse means that shipping data has been updated. People can read the same record and reach different conclusions. Shared context provides a foundation for handling the difference, but does not automatically create agreement about completion.

Defining the organization's outcome

Make a saved address change match a completed request

Return to the hypothetical delivery request. Identify the customer and order, check whether shipping status permits a change, and confirm the new address and the customer's intent. Save an allowed change to the relevant order and report the result. Updating the customer's profile while leaving this order addressed to the old location does not complete the request.

Evaluate a courteous explanation separately from what was saved to the order. Correctly declining an ineligible change, asking for missing address details, and respecting a withdrawn request also belong in the outcome criteria. Measuring only the share of requests automatically saved can encourage removal of necessary stops. Completion criteria must cover execution and stopping.

Conditions can change immediately before execution. If the warehouse ships an order after the initial check, permission based on the old shipping status is insufficient. One option is for the application or API responsible for saving to check the latest state and commit the change. An agent's understanding of the conditions does not establish safe handling of communication failures or concurrent updates.

Writing down those conditions makes smaller units of delegation possible. Checking an address and order and preparing a proposed change can be separated from saving it. A limited deployment can have value if that preparation reduces the employee's investigation. Giving the same execution permission across the organization need not be the first success criterion.

Ownership of exceptions

When the agent stops, the customer's request remains

If an order has shipped, customer support can explain alternatives. A question about warehouse handling goes to the warehouse. When a connection drops after a save and the result is unknown, a systems operator checks the saved outcome. These hypothetical exceptions all stop the agent, but leave different work to take over.

A single exit labeled “hand off to a human” is therefore incomplete. Pass the target order, what the customer confirmed, the last verified result, and what remains unknown in a form the receiving person can use. Treating an unknown result as non-execution and resending can create duplicate changes or notifications. Decide who checks the result, and how, before a retry.

Keep records to reduce that person's reinvestigation. Saving an entire conversation does not serve this purpose if the relevant order and completed change cannot be found. Extract the necessary target, confirmations, results, and gaps, and define who can view them and how long they are retained. More stored material does not necessarily make a handoff easier to use.

Responsibilities should also be assigned explicitly. The business owner defines eligibility; systems staff support saving and result checks; operations staff make exceptions receivable. Team sizes and organization charts can differ. The practical requirement is to know who explains a failure and who advances the remaining request.

Expanding adoption

Can the conditions verified in one trial transfer to the next department?

This article proposes three adoption checks. First, compare outcomes on prepared requests and states without changing production orders. Next, operate within a limited target using the verified combination of permissions and responsible staff. Before expanding, check whether the same completion and handoff conditions hold under another department's rules and destinations.

Check during the trial

Include ordinary requests, ineligible changes, missing details, withdrawal, and unknown results. Compare outcomes with fixed processing or human work.

Check before expansion

Revisit each department's rules, permissions, destinations, and exception owners. One successful department does not establish organization-wide readiness.

An address change supported in one service channel may have a different deadline or warehouse notification requirement in another. Similar tool names and request wording do not transfer the previous evaluation result. Add the differences to the evaluation and check that the actual receiving staff can handle handoffs. Expansion adds operating conditions as well as users.

These checks are a proposal, not universal maturity stages or numerical thresholds. The impact of an incorrect change, request volume, and receiving capacity affect the acceptable scope. Business owners need to define tolerable errors, waiting time, and unresolved outcomes and use them in the adoption decision. A high average score on another job cannot supply those conditions.

Cost and suitability

Measure the burden of finishing a request alongside automation volume

Even with a defined scope, the reason to use an agent deserves examination. Anthropic's implementation guidance distinguishes predefined workflows from agents in which the model chooses subsequent actions, and recommends choosing necessary complexity. When inputs are complete and a fixed form and API can enforce change conditions, that approach is a useful comparator.

Agent candidates include work that gathers missing information through conversation or organizes several requests. Model charges alone still give an incomplete cost comparison. Include review time, exception delays, correction work, and maintenance of rules and evaluations. If employees recheck everything despite increased automatic processing, the burden may simply have moved.

Conversely, a system where a person performs the final save can improve work if it organizes the target order, proposed change, and missing information and shortens review. Measure autonomy and business value separately. A narrower delegation is not itself failure; ask whether the receiving employee can advance the job.

The adoption decision

Design the delegated work and the work that comes back

Public evaluations show ways to check outcomes, while enterprise products add capabilities for business context and execution rules. These developments broaden the options for delegating bounded work. Which orders may change, what counts as completion, and who handles exceptions remain conditions to define and verify in the organization's own operations.

To move beyond the opening demo, test ineligible requests and executions with unknown results as well as repeated success. Check outcomes and permission, assign the remaining work, and measure the review burden. Delegate the jobs that meet those conditions and revisit them when the conditions change. The purpose is to make requests easier to carry through to completion while keeping ownership of the remaining work clear.
