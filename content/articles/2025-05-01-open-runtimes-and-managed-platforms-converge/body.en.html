Question

What is lost when connection, execution, and approval are treated as one “agent feature”?

Spring 2025 material from OpenAI, AWS, Google, and Microsoft appeared in different places: agent-building components, collaboration and human confirmation, multi-system agents and A2A, and Semantic Kernel Agents. It did not describe one finished common platform. This article treats protocol connectivity, managed execution, and approval for a change as separate responsibilities. That separation makes clear what can be exchanged and what organizational judgment remains.

Connection

Specify how tasks, tools, and artifacts move to another system, including the authentication scope.

Execution

Use models, tools, state, and coordination to progress work while retaining a record.

Approval

Decide who may authorize a change, what is checked, and where exceptions return.

Different responsibilities

A2A and managed platforms address different layers of the same design

Google’s A2A announcement proposed a protocol for agents to exchange tasks, state, and artifacts. It does not make connected systems share an execution platform or take responsibility for each other’s business policy. Google Cloud multi-system agents, AWS multi-agent collaboration, OpenAI agent-building tools, and Semantic Kernel Agents offer options for execution or coordination with distinct scopes and release states. They should not be placed on one maturity scale.

Research such as AutoGen, Magentic-One, and BrowserGym shows that multi-role or external-environment work needs observations, state, and completion conditions. A protocol makes it possible to call another participant; an execution platform makes progress possible. Neither decides whether customer data may be sent or a leave request may be approved.

Approval boundary

Bedrock confirmation and control return illustrate different responsibility for a change

AWS’s human-in-the-loop example describes confirmation before an agent executes an action group and a path that returns control to an application. Confirmation checks whether a user wants an action; it does not automatically satisfy an organization’s approval rule or the authorization of an external system.

For a leave request, an available HR API, an employee’s confirmation, approval under a manager’s policy, and a stored HR record are four different states. Keeping them separate prevents an access failure, a user cancellation, pending approval, and failed persistence from collapsing into one generic failure.

Hypothetical example

Follow a leave request through connection, execution, approval, and update

This is a design example. An agent checks leave balance and prepares a request. A2A or a tool contract specifies the role or API to call. Managed execution can retain the request, state, and response. User confirmation checks dates and reason. Managerial approval applies organizational policy. Only a verified HR-system record completes the update.

Each stage needs defined input, permission, result, and destination after failure. Changing a connection format does not transfer approval rules or storage consistency automatically. Keeping completion conditions stable across execution platforms makes later architecture changes easier to test.

Conclusion

A shared design diagram should place responsibility boundaries, not product names, at its center

The releases made more separate components for connection, execution, coordination, and confirmation available. Their value is not evidence that interoperability is complete. It is a way to separate connection contracts, execution records, and approval responsibility, then explicitly preserve the judgments that must remain with people and organizations.
